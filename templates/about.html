About

A low-cost optical eardrum sensor and two models: a clinical model on public data, and a hardware model for our own recordings.

The idea

Intracranial pressure (ICP) is normally measured with a probe through the skull. The eardrum is coupled to the fluid around the brain through the inner ear, so an optical sensor at the eardrum may respond when ICP changes. We tested what a low-cost version of that can and cannot show.

Data

  • CHARIS (PhysioNet, public): 13 brain-injury patients, invasive ICP, 915,137 ten-second windows.
  • Our hardware recordings: 146 volunteers (ages 7 to 83, median 21; 107 male, 39 female), four sessions each in a fixed order: supine, head-up 30°, head-down 10°, Valsalva. 46,407 windows at 50 Hz. Sensor: ESP32 with an infrared optical sensor, a 16-bit ADC and a motion sensor.

Method

  • Artefact-flagged rows removed, 10-second windows with 50% overlap, flat windows dropped, detrended.
  • Hardware model: 32 optical features from the IR, displacement and red channels (the motion sensor is not used), XGBoost, trained on the recorded manoeuvre labels.
  • Every metric uses unseen subjects; thresholds are chosen on separate validation subjects.

Limits

  • The hardware model recognises the recorded state; it is not an ICP measurement, is not calibrated to mmHg, and there are no patients with raised ICP in the hardware data.
  • Manoeuvre order was fixed. Checks against slow drift and body movement are on the Results page, but a randomised order would settle it.
  • IR and red channels alone each carry most of the signal, so systemic blood-flow changes with posture and straining are part of what is recognised.
  • The Valsalva block in each recording is several minutes long, while a single Valsalva effort lasts seconds, so some windows labelled Valsalva may be rest.
  • The CHARIS model does not transfer to the hardware after five alignment attempts.
